Al Mansub

Just as spoken language has its dialects, so too does the written word.
The proportional Arabic script developed over centuries across several geographic,
spiritual, and political centers.
At ALMANSUB.design, you can trace the evolution of the script through a comparison of styles, historical artifacts, and formal principles.
The project reveals the nuances of how form developed across different traditions, cultures, and eras the forces that shaped one of the world's most influential writing systems.

01

Home

Most people look at Arabic script and see decoration something beautiful, ornamental, to be admired rather than read. But beneath that beauty is an exact engineering system: a method born in 10th-century Baghdad where every letter was measured against a single rhombic dot. ALMANASUB is an interactive platform that reveals this hidden system, and traces how it traveled across the Islamic world mutating into distinct scripts in every culture it reached.

02

History

The history page, built as scrollytelling, Scroll drives the narrative from the first revealed word, Iqra, (read) through the early script's development toward the proportional system.

03

Map

An interactive map placing each script tradition in its geographic center, connecting form to place.

04

The 7 scripts

The comparison screen, showing the same word written in all six classical scripts at once. A toggle reveals the coral nuqta beneath each one, exposing the shared proportional system every script rests on the common skeleton behind their different forms.

05

Script page

An inner script page the detailed view of a single script. It follows the same scrollytelling structure throughout: its origin, technical character, alphabet, letter anatomy, and real historical examples, letting the viewer study one script in depth.

06

Letter anatomy

A close up animation of a single letter being written with the qalam, revealing its anatomy and proportions. It shows the script not as a finished form but as a constructed one each stroke measured against the nuqta.

The Problem

The history of Islamic calligraphy is well documented but almost entirely in academic form: dense, text-heavy, and inaccessible to anyone outside the field. And its visual language assumes you already read Arabic.

I set out to solve a design problem: how do you translate an ancient, mathematical system of proportion into a visual experience that anyone including someone who has never read a word of Arabic can understand and feel?

Research

The project rests on primary sources manuscripts and acadime researches and on conversations with a scholar and a practicing calligrapher, who helped me verify both the history and the mechanics of the script.
Beyond gathering images, the research meant getting the history right, Holding the distinctions rather than flattening them into a tidy story was central to the project's integrity.
